    Reading five controls on two analog pins A2 · 02

    The interesting part of this board isn't the buttons — it's that six rotary positions and two independent levers are each read through a single MCU analog input, by putting the switching inside a resistor ladder instead of on the pin count.

    A1 · Every resistor on the back · eight parts · two jobs
    Five controls reach the Nano on two analogue pins. Both pins are A6 and A7, which on the TQFP‑32 ATmega328P are ADC inputs with no digital driver and no internal pull‑up. They can never do anything else — which is exactly why ladders belong on them, and why spending real GPIO or an I²C expander here would be paying for a shortage that does not exist.

    R1–R5 — the A6 tapped divider A1

    Five 4.7 kΩ in series turn the rail into six voltages, and the wiper picks one. That is the whole circuit. The taps land on 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 V, so the ADC reads 0, 205, 409, 614, 818, 1023 — about 204 codes between neighbours.

    1% parts move a tap by roughly two codes, so the margin is about a hundredfold. The tolerance is not there for accuracy; it is there so one firmware threshold table fits all ten units without calibrating each clock by hand.

    The string is 23.5 kΩ and draws 213 µA continuously. The number that actually constrains the design is source impedance: the ADC's sample‑and‑hold wants to see under 10 kΩ, and the worst tap looks back into two resistors in parallel with three — 9.4 k ∥ 14.1 k = 5.64 kΩ. Comfortable.

    MODE rotary → A6 A2 · 6-position tapped divider

    R1–R5 (4.7k 1%) chain from GND to +5V. The rotary wiper lands on one of six nodes along that chain, so each position presents a distinct, evenly-spaced voltage on A6 — no separate pin per position.

    R6, R7, R8 — the A7 lever ladder A1

    Two switches, four states, one pin. R6 holds A7 at the rail when both levers are open. Closing a lever hangs its resistor from A7 to ground, and the two values are chosen so no combination collides:

    FIELDSUBTo groundADC
    openopen—1023
    closedopen20 k682
    openclosed10 k512
    closedclosed6.67 k409

    Worst gap is 103 codes, between the last two rows — still far more than any tolerance stack will move. Worst‑case draw is 300 µA with both closed.

    The levers switch to ground, never to the rail. A closed switch then clamps the pin hard to 0 V through a low resistance, which is the state most immune to what a metre of cable picks up running past a converter chopping 185 V at 31 kHz.

    seen The converter runs at 31 kHz (firmware 1_setup.ino). The lead is 180–200 mm, not a metre (TS06-DRV bom.md); switching to ground helps all the same inferred.

    FIELD + SUB → A7 A2 · binary-weighted 2-lever encode

    A7 has its own 10k pull-up to +5V (R6). Lever A pulls it down through R7 (20k); lever B pulls it down through R8 (10k) — different values on purpose, so each of the four lever combinations divides the pull-up to a different, distinguishable voltage.

    The two resistors that are not here A1

    The buttons have no pull‑ups on this board, because the ATmega has its own and the firmware already switches them on. Two parts saved, and a shorter cable.

    The two 100 nF filter caps are deliberately at the main board end, not here. They do two jobs that only work at that end: they hold the last reading when a non‑shorting wiper floats mid‑rotation, and they shunt cable pickup to ground right at the ADC pin. Put them on the fascia and the whole cable still hangs off the pin unfiltered.

    With 5.64 kΩ of source impedance that cap forms a 564 µs low‑pass — slow against noise, fast against a hand turning a knob, and well inside the firmware's five‑identical‑readings filter at 10 ms.

    Why the ladder earns the board A1

    Without it, five controls need five conductors plus power and ground — seven wires, and two of the Nano's pins that could have driven something. With it, the count is six wires and zero real GPIO, because A6 and A7 were never capable of anything else.

    Eight resistors cost about 30 ₽. That is the entire price of collapsing the panel down to one connector, and it is why this board is worth fabricating even before you count the printed face.

    The resistors sit on the back, behind the switches they belong to. Anchoring them at copper pads rather than leaving them in free air on flying leads is the difference between a panel that still reads true in month three and one that drifts as oxidised joints move the divider.

      What the controls do A1 · turn the dial — the panel legend cannot

      The rotary has six fixed names, so it letters itself. The levers do not: each means something different on every screen, which is why the face says FIELD and SUB — the one thing about them that stays true. Pick a position and watch what a printed label would have had to promise.

      SUB now has one rule, and it holds everywhere: it is read only when FIELD is thrown to its second position — Effects on Display, Date on Format/Date. Two screens, one grammar, no exception to letter. The face draws that rule as a supply: a gold trace ends directly beneath FIELD, at the point its second throw reaches, and runs from there into the box enclosing SUB. Two more leave that box for positions 3 and 5. Nothing reaches SUB except through that point, so the panel says this is live when the lever comes down here without lettering a word of it — and the lever itself, not a drawing of one, closes the gap.

      Planned, not yet in the firmware seen. The screens below are control scheme rev B, the spec. Today’s firmware (firmware/nixieClock_TS06/ts06pair.ino) reads the rotary only as SET TIME = PROGRAM and the other five positions = RUN, the two-button interface of the old boards; it does not read A7, so FIELD and SUB do nothing yet; and it keeps the ИН-15 pair dark, so NORMAL’s idle cycle is not written. Its own TODO lists the six screens.

      05 · Routing & the GND pour · A2

      The SMD build's one hard rule: nothing functional ever touches the front. That rule shapes everything below it — and one placement choice almost broke the ground plane before placement itself fixed it.

      64 signal tracks, one layer, zero vias — anywhere

      Every non-GND net routes entirely on B.Cu. F.Cu carries only the decorative gold artwork, so a signal trace can never physically collide with it — there's no layer transition to get wrong, and none was needed.

      R6 was sitting in the one corridor everything else needed

      R6 (the A7 pull-up) originally sat directly between R7 and R8, in the same narrow stretch the +5V, A6, A7, D7 and D8 traces all had to cross to reach J1. That walled three GND pads (SW4.1, R8.2, R7.2) off from the rest of the plane — no B.Cu path existed at any trace width down to the clearance minimum, checked exhaustively before concluding this.

      Moving it — nothing else — closed the gap

      R6 moved out of that corridor; no other component, trace width, or clearance setting changed. That alone opened enough room for the GND pour to reach all seven pads on its own, verified against KiCad's own computed filled_polygon data (not just this repo's simulated fill): one connected region, every GND pad inside it. No stitch traces, no vias.

      Open before Gerbers · A1 · a respin costs 15 000 ₽ and a week

      Closed — twelve detents, so the step is exactly 30.00°

      Counted, not measured. A count is an integer, so this is now exact rather than estimated: 360 ÷ 12 = 30.00° per step, six positions spanning 150°. It also agrees with the twelve outer taps the model already carried, which is the same claim arriving from a second direction. The earlier ~32°/160° figure was an eye estimate of a swept arc and is withdrawn.

      Does the bushing clear the stack?

      The diameters are measured and settled. What is not settled is the stack behind them: 7.00 mm of usable bushing, 2.0 mm of FR4, and the fascia recessed into the chassis — so nut, washer and recess depth all have to fit in the 5.00 mm that is left. That is a depth question against the printed chassis, not a diameter question.

      seen Today: still open. PCB/TS06-FASCIA-variants.md asks, whichever variant is chosen, for a dry fit of a real КМД1 and МТ1 against the panel.

      Resolved — SUB now has one rule

      Giving SUB the glitch rate on Display makes it live on two screens instead of one, and both obey the same condition: FIELD thrown to its second position. The face draws that as a supply: a gold trace ending directly under FIELD where its second throw lands, running into the box around SUB, with two leaving for positions 3 and 5. Nothing reaches SUB except through that point — the rule stated as geometry rather than as a caption, and closed by the real lever rather than a picture of one.

      seen Today: the board draws it (the gold box and traces in the render above). The firmware does not read SUB yet.

      Resolved — INFO moved to position 6

      Both parking screens now sit on end stops. NORMAL at one extreme, INFO at the other, and every live editing screen in between. The knob cannot be knocked past either end into something that changes a setting. Firmware cost is one row of the A6 threshold table: 818 → Format/Date, 1023 → Info.

      seen Today: the silkscreen reads NORMAL … INFO in this order, and the firmware’s comment maps 818 to FORMAT/DATE and 1023 to INFO.

      That figure was bad caliper technique and has been re‑measured at 25.00 mm. It has already propagated into spec §2, §6's panel table, three header notes and prototype plan P1/P8. Those still say something false, and §1's control table now does too.

      Loading the order sheet…

      2 · Bare boards, on arrival
      1. Look them over against the renders. Under a loupe, check the strip rows XS21, XS23, XS24, XS25 and XP21, XP23, XP24, XP25: a 5 V LED line sits 0.84 mm from a 185 V anode pad there. Check VT21's three pads and the empty bleed footprints R33–R44 (0.94 mm).
      2. Meter on its highest range: every high-to-low pair in the guide's table 1b reads open. +12V, +5V and HV185 are each open to GND.
      3. Build, then solder the strips with the two boards screwed together on their nylon standoffs, so all 63 strip pins line up (Build steps 3–4 above).
      4. Before any power, with the power off: turn RP1 until pin 1 to pin 2 (GND) reads 5.0 kΩ, its maximum. That is the lowest set-point, 165 V. C7+ to U12 pin 2 must read 560–615 kΩ; 963 kΩ means R62 or R63 is open, and then the converter must not be powered.
      What you need
      • A 0–15 V bench supply with a current limit, at least 1 A.
      • A meter rated CAT II 600 V DC or better, 10 MΩ input, with insulated micro-grabber clips.
      • A plastic trimming tool for RP1, and a stopwatch.
      • A 100 kΩ 1 W resistor on insulated clip leads, for draining C7.
      • An LED probe (3 mm LED + 1 kΩ) and a stand-in lamp (100 kΩ with two Dupont pins).
      • The bring-up sketch firmware/ts06_bringup (9600 baud), then the clock at BOARD_TYPE 4.
      3 · Bench bring-up, one stage at a time
      185 V DC, stored in C7 (4.7 µF, 0.08 J), can hurt you. With high voltage on: one hand only; clip the meter on with the power off and C7 under 10 V, then power up; never move a probe on a live board. After power-off, wait 15 s and check C7 reads under 10 V before a hand goes near the board. A scope's ground clip goes only to board GND. Fit and pull chips only with the power off.

        This page shows exported models. The real files open in KiCad 10, where you can click any track, see its net, hide layers and run the same design-rule check. KiCad 9 and older cannot open them.

        Get the files and open them
        1. Install KiCad 10 from kicad.org/download, with its 3D models (on Linux they are often a separate package, kicad-packages3d).
        2. Clone the branch: 
          git clone -b pcb/kicad-boards \
  https://github.com/BobStolb/TERMINAL-06-firmware
cd TERMINAL-06-firmware
        3. File → Open Project: PCB/TS06-DISP/TS06-DISP.kicad_pro, then PCB/TS06-DRV/TS06-DRV.kicad_pro. Double-click the .kicad_pcb to open the PCB Editor. Each project carries its own library table, so the TS06 footprints in PCB/lib load with no setup.
        4. There is no schematic file for the pair: its netlist is tools/ts06pair.py, and the generators write the boards from it. The Circuit sections tab draws it section by section.
        5. Do not save the boards. Refilling or running DRC changes the board in memory; answer Discard when KiCad asks. If you saved by accident: git checkout -- PCB/.
        Inside the PCB editor
        1. Fill the pours first: press B, or Edit → Fill All Zones. The committed boards store no fill, so the ground pours look empty until you do, and the display's LED return (BL_K) exists only as a pour.
        2. Faces: on TS06-DISP the tubes are on F, the pin strips XP11–XP25 on B. On TS06-DRV the parts are on F (towards the rear panel) and the socket strips XS11–XS25 on B (towards the display). View → Flip Board View shows the back.
        3. Layers: the Appearance panel's eye icons; Ctrl+H dims all but the active layer; PgUp/PgDn switch F.Cu and B.Cu.
        4. Nets: hover a pad to read its net; ` (back-quote) lights the whole net. Try HV185, the 185 V net, and note the 0.6 mm gaps of the HV class.
        5. Find: Ctrl+F finds C7, RP1 or XS21. Pin 1 is the square pad.
        6. DRC: Inspect → Design Rules Checker, tick Refill all zones, Run.
          • TS06-DISP: 0 unconnected; 2 errors, accepted (the colon lamp courtyards overlap M10 by 0.135 mm).
          • TS06-DRV: 0 errors, 0 unconnected, 4 accepted warnings (the Nano's silk past the edge ×2; VT21 and XS1 library mismatches).
        7. 3D: Alt+3. Drag to turn, scroll to zoom.
        For the fab: refill before plotting

        The boards are ordered 1.6 mm FR4 (the fascia 2.0 mm), black soldermask, white silkscreen, ENIG. The committed files store no zone fill, so Gerbers plotted straight from them would have no ground pours and no BL_K return. Refill first:

        kicad-cli pcb export gerbers --check-zones -o gerbers/ PCB/TS06-DRV/TS06-DRV.kicad_pcb
